Barend Nooren

I am a GIS and sustainability student at Utrecht University, interested in cartography, spatial data science, and using geospatial technologies to better understand environmental and social challenges.

My work focuses on applying GIS methods to real-world questions — from mapping global undernourishment patterns aligned with UN SDG indicators, to analysing local school accessibility in Utrecht using OpenStreetMap data and routing algorithms.

I enjoy working at the intersection of data, design, and geography — turning complex spatial datasets into clear, communicative maps.

Urban Flood Risk Analysis — The Hague

🎯 SDG Goal 11 — Sustainable Cities and Communities · Target 11.5

This project analyses urban flood risk in The Hague using high-resolution LiDAR elevation data from the AHN (Actueel Hoogtebestand Nederland). A Digital Terrain Model (DTM) was used to identify low-lying areas most vulnerable to inundation under a 1-metre flood scenario.

Buildings were extruded by height using Zonal Statistics, revealing the 3D urban fabric in relation to flood exposure. The analysis highlights coastal and city-centre neighbourhoods where ground elevation falls below or near sea level — a critical issue for a low-lying Dutch city.

This work connects directly to SDG 11.5, which aims to reduce the impact of disasters including flooding on urban communities by 2030.

AHN (Actueel Hoogtebestand Nederland)
QGIS, Raster Calculator, Zonal Statistics
The Hague, Netherlands

Vegetation Change Monitoring — Singapore & Johor Bahru

🎯 SDG Goal 15 — Life on Land · Monitoring Terrestrial Ecosystem Change

This project uses multispectral satellite imagery from Sentinel-2 to monitor vegetation change between 2017 and 2024 across Singapore and the southern Malaysian state of Johor Bahru — an area that juxtaposes a densely urbanised city-state with a rapidly developing region where natural forests are giving way to oil palm plantations and urban expansion.

NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) composites were generated in Google Earth Engine using full-year median Sentinel-2 imagery to eliminate cloud cover. NDVI was then calculated in QGIS using the formula (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red), where high values indicate dense healthy vegetation and negative values indicate water or bare surfaces.

Data source
Copernicus / Sentinel-2 (ESA) via Google Earth Engine
Tools used
Google Earth Engine, QGIS, Raster Calculator
Study period
2017 vs 2024 (full-year medians)
Study area
Singapore & Johor Bahru, Malaysia
Map 1

True Colour Composite — 2017

Singapore's compact urban footprint is visible surrounded by water, with vegetated areas in the central nature reserve. Across the Johor Strait, Johor Bahru's urban core gives way to a mosaic of oil palm, secondary forest, and agricultural land. Construction sites appear as pale grey-beige patches.

Map 2

True Colour Composite — 2024

By 2024, notable expansion is visible in Johor, particularly around the Iskandar Malaysia economic corridor. New infrastructure and housing have replaced vegetation visible in 2017. Land reclamation on Singapore's southern coast is visible. The contrast between urban Singapore and transitional Johor is more pronounced.

Map 3

NDVI — 2017

High vegetation density (NDVI > 0.4) across much of Johor's interior. Singapore's central nature reserve stands out as an isolated high-NDVI patch within an urban surface. The Johor Strait shows negative NDVI values (water), and urban surfaces throughout both areas show near-zero values.

Map 4

NDVI — 2024

A broadly similar pattern to 2017, but with notable shifts. Some Johor areas show lower NDVI consistent with conversion to built-up land. Others show higher values — likely reflecting maturation of oil palm replanted after 2015. Singapore's vegetation patches remain relatively stable.

Map 5

NDVI Change — 2017 → 2024

The change map (2024 NDVI minus 2017 NDVI) reveals where vegetation increased (green) and decreased (red/orange) over seven years. Red patches around Johor Bahru's urban fringe indicate active deforestation and land conversion.

Green areas in Johor's interior largely reflect oil palm plantation regrowth — not natural forest recovery. Singapore's protected nature areas remain stable, while development frontiers show slight negative change.

This map illustrates the complex relationship between vegetation density and land use: not all green gain is ecologically positive, and not all red loss is permanent.

Land Use / Land Cover Classification — Utrecht

🎯 SDG Goal 15 — Life on Land · Monitoring Terrestrial Ecosystems

Land cover classification maps what covers the Earth's surface — forests, water, urban areas and agricultural fields — using satellite imagery. This project applies three classification approaches to a Sentinel-2 composite of Utrecht, comparing unsupervised and supervised methods to assess their accuracy and suitability for land cover monitoring.

The imagery was processed in ArcGIS Pro using the Image Classification Wizard. Each pixel represents approximately 18 × 18 metres. A Support Vector Machine (SVM) supervised classifier — trained on manually drawn samples for five land cover classes — achieved a cross-validation accuracy of 99.53%, significantly outperforming both unsupervised methods.

Data source
Sentinel-2 (ESA Copernicus) · May/June 2026
Tools used
ArcGIS Pro · Image Classification Wizard · SVM
Classes mapped
Water · Developed · Barren · Forest · Planted/Cultivated
Best accuracy
99.53% (SVM cross-validation)
Map 1

True Colour Composite — Sentinel-2

Natural colour Sentinel-2 image (bands B4/B3/B2) of the Utrecht study area. Clearly shows the urban core, agricultural fields, and vegetated areas. Forms the base imagery for all three classification methods.

Map 2

NDVI — Vegetation Health Index

Red-to-green colour ramp: green indicates healthy dense vegetation, red indicates bare soil or urban surfaces. Agricultural fields and parks are clearly distinguishable from the built-up city centre. Ground truth validation points are overlaid in red.

Map 3

Unsupervised Pixel-Based Classification

ISO Cluster algorithm groups pixels into 4 classes by spectral similarity alone — no training data required. Vegetation, urban areas and water are distinguishable, but the results are coarse and some land cover types that should be separate are merged into one class.

Map 4

Unsupervised Object-Based Classification

Groups neighbouring pixels into objects before classification, producing smoother and more natural-looking results. Fields and urban blocks are more accurately delineated compared to pixel-based classification, though manual interpretation of class labels is still required afterwards.

Map 5 · Best Result

Supervised Pixel-Based Classification (SVM)

Using training samples manually drawn for five classes — Water, Developed, Barren, Forest, and Planted/Cultivated — the Support Vector Machine classifier produced the most detailed and accurate result. Cross-validation accuracy: 99.53%.

The SVM successfully separated vegetation subtypes that both unsupervised methods merged into one class. Some urban pixels were occasionally misclassified as Barren due to similar spectral signatures — a known limitation when surface materials share reflectance properties.

💧 Water 🏙️ Developed 🟤 Barren 🌲 Forest 🌾 Planted/Cultivated
Chart

Spectral Profile — Training Samples

The spectral profile shows how each land cover type reflects light across Sentinel-2's 12 bands. These characteristic "spectral signatures" are what enable classification algorithms to distinguish between land cover types.

Vegetation shows a distinctive NIR peak (B8), while water has uniformly low reflectance and urban surfaces show a flat, moderate response. The separation between these signatures is what makes SVM classification so effective.
